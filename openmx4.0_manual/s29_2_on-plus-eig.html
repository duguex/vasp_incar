29.2 Combination of the O(N) Method and the Conventional Eigenvalue Solver

Large-scale systems containing more than 1000 atoms can be treated by the O(N) method, but the O(N) method implemented in OpenMX does not provide information on the wave functions. A simple way to obtain wave functions and the corresponding eigenvalues for large-scale systems is to first use the O(N) method to obtain the self-consistent charge density, and then perform the conventional diagonalization calculation only once under this self-consistent charge density. As an example of this method, we show a large-scale calculation for a multiply connected carbon nanotube (MCCN) consisting of 564 carbon atoms. First, an SCF calculation for MCCN was carried out using 16 MPI processes (Xeon, 2.6GHz) and the O(N) Krylov subspace method. The computational conditions were as follows: C5.0-s2p1 (basis functions), scf.energycutoff=130 Ryd, scf.criterion=1.0e-7, orderN.HoppingRanges=6.5 Å, orderN.KrylovH.order=400, and RMM-DIISK (mixing method). The input file is MCCN.dat in the work directory. The norm of the difference charge density in the Fourier representation is shown as a function of the SCF step in Fig. 31. It took 56 SCF steps for the charge density of the system to converge, and the computational time was about 7 minutes. After that, the following keywords are set.

    scf.maxIter                1
    scf.EigenvalueSolver    Band
    scf.Kgrid              1 1 1
    scf.restart               on
    MO.fileout                on
    num.HOMOs                  2
    num.LUMOs                  2
    MO.Nkpoint                 1
    <MO.kpoint
      0.0  0.0  0.0
    MO.kpoint>

Next, in order to calculate the wave functions, a calculation for the same system was carried out using 16 MPI processes (Xeon, 2.6GHz) and the conventional diagonalization method, and the computational time was about 2 minutes. Figure 32 shows the isosurfaces of the HOMO and LUMO at the Γ point of MCCN calculated by the above method. In this calculation example, the difference in the computational time between the O(N) method and the conventional diagonalization method is not so large, but this approach, which combines the O(N) method and the conventional eigenvalue solver, is considered to be useful for large-scale systems containing several thousand atoms.

16.1 SCF convergence basics

In a self-consistent field (SCF) calculation, the output obtained by solving the Kohn-Sham equations from a given input is iterated until it becomes consistent with the input used for the next iteration. If the quantity given as input is written as Xn(in) and the output obtained from it as Xn(out), the goal of SCF convergence is to find a fixed point satisfying

Xn(out)=Xn(in) (3)

In practice, SCF is regarded as converged when the residual

Rn=Xn(out)−Xn(in) (4)

becomes sufficiently small.

An important point here is that the quantity X to be mixed is not always the same. In OpenMX, seven methods are implemented depending on the quantity to be mixed. The first point to understand is that there are three categories:

  • •

    methods that mix the density matrix,

  • •

    methods that mix the electron density in Fourier space,

  • •

    methods that mix the Kohn-Sham potential or the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian.

Keeping this distinction in mind makes it easier to understand the role of each keyword and what should be changed when convergence is not achieved.

Summary of the mixing methods available in OpenMX

Table 4 summarizes the mixing methods available in OpenMX. In the first three methods (Simple, GR-Pulay, and RMM-DIIS), the density matrix is mainly mixed. Here, the density matrix is a matrix represented in localized basis functions, from which the electron density is calculated. On the other hand, in Kerker and RMM-DIISK, the electron density represented in Fourier space is directly mixed. This distinction is important for understanding SCF convergence in metallic systems. In metals, long-wavelength charge fluctuations (charge sloshing) hinder convergence, and it is effective to suppress small-wavevector components appropriately in Fourier space [62, 61]. Furthermore, in RMM-DIISV and RMM-DIISH, the quantity to be mixed is not the electron density itself, but the Kohn-Sham potential or the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian.


What an SCF calculation is intended to achieve

When the density matrix is mixed, the Kohn-Sham equations are solved using the input density matrix ρn(in) at the nth iteration, and the output density matrix ρn(out) is obtained. In this case, the goal of SCF convergence is

ρn(out)=ρn(in) (5)

Therefore, the residual is defined by

Rn=ρn(out)−ρn(in) (6)

In methods that mix the electron density in Fourier space, we consider the Fourier transforms n~n(in)⁢(𝐪) and n~n(out)⁢(𝐪) of the input electron density nn(in)⁢(𝐫) and the output electron density nn(out)⁢(𝐫), and the residual is

Rn⁢(𝐪)=n~n(out)⁢(𝐪)−n~n(in)⁢(𝐪) (7)

Thus, the first step in understanding SCF convergence schemes is to be aware of what quantity is being mixed. The quantity NormRD output in the System.Name.out file also denotes the norm of the residual for the density matrix, electron density, potential, or Hamiltonian, depending on the mixing method.

Simple mixing

The most basic method is simple mixing (Simple). This method constructs the next input by linearly interpolating only the previous input and output [61, 65]. Written for the density matrix,

ρn+1(in)=α⁢ρn(in)+(1−α)⁢ρn(out) (8)

Here, α is the mixing ratio. Since Simple mixing uses information from only the immediately preceding step, it may work well enough for molecules or insulators with a large gap, but in metallic systems or large-scale systems convergence is often slow or tends to oscillate.

Pulay-type mixing: GR-Pulay and the RMM-DIIS family

The essential difference between Simple mixing and the RMM-DIIS family is that Simple mixing uses only the previous input and output, whereas Pulay-type mixing uses the history of multiple previous steps. Using this history is based on an idea similar to a quasi-Newton method, in the sense that it chooses a direction that makes the residual as small as possible. The “approximate Hessian” here means an approximation, obtained from the history, to how the residual changes in response to changes in the input, and in Pulay-type mixing this information is used implicitly to determine the update direction.

In RMM-DIIS, one first defines the residual

Rn=ρn(out)−ρn(in) (9)

and approximates the residual in the next SCF step by a linear combination of the previous p steps,

R¯n+1=∑m=n−(p−1)nam⁢Rm (10)

Then, under the constraint

∑m=n−(p−1)nam=1 (11)

the coefficients am are chosen so that the residual norm

⟨R¯n+1|R¯n+1⟩ (12)

is minimized [61]. As a result, the next input is constructed as

ρn+1(in)=∑m=n−(p−1)nam⁢ρm(in) (13)

That is, the essence of the RMM-DIIS family is to choose, from the past history, the direction that most effectively reduces the residual.

GR-Pulay is likewise a Pulay-type mixing method that uses history, but it is designed so that the residual decreases reliably at each update [60]. RMM-DIIS, RMM-DIISK, RMM-DIISV, and RMM-DIISH are all Pulay-type mixing methods, but they differ in what quantity is mixed.

Kerker mixing and RMM-DIISK

In metallic systems and small-gap systems, charge sloshing, in which the long-wavelength component of the electron density oscillates strongly, hinders SCF convergence. Kerker mixing suppresses this long-wavelength component in Fourier space [62]. For the Fourier components of the electron-density difference, one may typically write

δ⁢n~n+1(in)⁢(𝐪)=α⁢w⁢(𝐪)⁢δ⁢n~n(in)⁢(𝐪)+[1−α⁢w⁢(𝐪)]⁢δ⁢n~n(out)⁢(𝐪) (14)

Here, the Kerker weight is given by

w⁢(𝐪)=|𝐪|2|𝐪|2+q02, (15)
q0=αK⁢|𝐪min| (16)

Since w⁢(𝐪) becomes small for small |𝐪|, excessive updates of the long-wavelength components are suppressed.

RMM-DIISK is a method that incorporates the Kerker idea into Pulay-type mixing. That is, for the residual in Fourier space,

Rn⁢(𝐪)=n~n(out)⁢(𝐪)−n~n(in)⁢(𝐪) (17)

the residual norm is minimized using the Kerker metric

⟨A|B⟩=∑𝐪A𝐪∗⁢B𝐪w⁢(𝐪) (18)

[61, 62]. Therefore, it is useful to understand that

  • •

    Kerker is “Simple mixing in Fourier space,”

  • •

    RMM-DIISK is “Pulay-type mixing in Fourier space.”

The reason why RMM-DIISK is the first choice in many systems is that it combines both suppression of charge sloshing and acceleration by using the history.

RMM-DIISV and RMM-DIISH

RMM-DIISV is a Pulay-type mixing method in which the Kohn-Sham potential, rather than the electron density itself, is the quantity to be mixed. On the other hand, RMM-DIISH directly mixes the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian matrix. This difference is particularly important in the plus U method and in calculations with constraints. In ordinary LDA or GGA, the Kohn-Sham potential is given as a functional of the electron density. Therefore, if the electron density or density matrix is mixed appropriately, the potential and Hamiltonian also become consistent indirectly. However, the situation is different in the plus U method and in constraint methods. In these methods, terms depending on the occupation-number matrix or local density matrix are added to the Hamiltonian. Therefore, the Hamiltonian is no longer a functional of only the total electron density. For example, in the plus U method, the energy is conceptually written as

E=EDFT⁢[n]+EU⁢[ρ] (19)

Thus, it depends not only on the electron density n, but also on the occupation-number matrix ρ. For this reason, methods that mix only the electron density may not update the Hamiltonian contribution originating from the U term sufficiently. As a result, mixing only the electron density may be insufficient to update the Hamiltonian containing the U term or constraint terms, and even when the electron density appears to change only slightly, part of the Hamiltonian matrix may still fluctuate strongly. For this reason, in the plus U method or in constraint methods, RMM-DIISH, which mixes the Hamiltonian matrix itself from the history as

Hn+1(in)←∑m=n−(p−1)nam⁢Hm(in) (20)

can be effective. In other words, the reason why RMM-DIISH works well with the plus U method and with constraint methods is that it can directly stabilize not only the electron density but also the Hamiltonian itself including auxiliary degrees of freedom.

How to choose a mixing method

A common dead end is to change many keywords simultaneously from the beginning, so that one can no longer tell what actually helped. We first recommend the following policy:

  1. 1.

    For ordinary molecules, insulators, and standard band calculations, first try RMM-DIISK.

  2. 2.

    For metallic systems, surface systems, and large clusters as well, first try RMM-DIISK.

  3. 3.

    For the plus U method and calculations with constraints, try RMM-DIISH from the outset.

  4. 4.

    Only when convergence is still unstable, try Kerker alone.

In general, many mixing methods can achieve convergence in systems with a large gap. However, in small-gap systems and metallic systems, charge sloshing tends to become serious, and Kerker, RMM-DIISK, and RMM-DIISV are effective. In addition, in the plus U method and in constraint methods, RMM-DIISH may be a more natural choice.

From Fig. 8, one can see that RMM-DIISK and RMM-DIISV work robustly for many systems. Therefore, in ordinary calculations it is practical to regard RMM-DIISK as the first choice, and in the plus U method or in constraint methods to try RMM-DIISH.

Practical tuning procedure

For beginners, we recommend tuning in the following order.

  1. 1.

    First use RMM-DIISK and do not specify scf.Kerker.factor. In this case, the Kerker factor is automatically estimated.

  2. 2.

    If convergence is slow, increase scf.Mixing.History to about 30–40.

  3. 3.

    If divergence or large oscillations are observed, increase scf.Mixing.StartPulay from 10 to about 30.

  4. 4.

    In metallic systems or difficult cases, increase scf.ElectronicTemperature.

  5. 5.

    In the plus U method or in constraint methods, switch to RMM-DIISH.

  6. 6.

    If it is still unstable, manually set scf.Kerker.factor.

For scf.Kerker.factor, first use the automatically determined value. Since this value depends on the system size, the anisotropy of the reciprocal lattice vectors, and the degree of metallicity, reusing a value that worked for another system without modification may slow convergence or even make it unstable. First use the automatically estimated value, and adjust it manually only when it is insufficient.

Typical setting examples

An example of settings to try first for many systems is shown below.

scf.Mixing.Type           rmm-diisk
scf.Init.Mixing.Weight    0.02
scf.Min.Mixing.Weight     0.01
scf.Max.Mixing.Weight     0.30
scf.Mixing.History        30
scf.Mixing.StartPulay     15
scf.Mixing.EveryPulay     1
scf.ElectronicTemperature 300.0
scf.criterion             1.0e-7

At this stage, do not specify scf.Kerker.factor; leave it to the automatic estimation. For metallic systems that are more difficult to converge, for example, the following settings may be used as a starting point.

scf.Mixing.Type           rmm-diisk
scf.Init.Mixing.Weight    0.01
scf.Min.Mixing.Weight     0.01
scf.Max.Mixing.Weight     0.20
scf.Mixing.History        40
scf.Mixing.StartPulay     20
scf.Mixing.EveryPulay     1
scf.ElectronicTemperature 1000.0
scf.criterion             1.0e-7

Only when convergence is still insufficient should scf.Kerker.factor be specified manually. However, if the value is made too large, the long-wavelength components are strongly suppressed, but convergence may become slow.

In the plus U method or in calculations with constraints, it is a good idea to try RMM-DIISH as follows.

scf.Mixing.Type           rmm-diish
scf.Init.Mixing.Weight    0.02
scf.Mixing.History        20
scf.Mixing.StartPulay     10
scf.criterion             1.0e-7

If Hamiltonian mixing seems too strong, switch to RMM-DIIS and try density-matrix mixing.

Role of the main keywords

scf.Mixing.Type
Specifies the mixing method and the quantity to be mixed. For many systems, RMM-DIISK is the first choice. In the plus U method and in calculations with constraints, RMM-DIISH or RMM-DIIS is also a strong candidate.

scf.Init.Mixing.Weight
Specifies the initial mixing ratio. A value that is too large causes divergence or oscillation. For beginners, it is safe to start from about 0.01–0.05.

scf.Min.Mixing.Weight
The lower bound of the mixing ratio in Simple mixing and Kerker mixing.

scf.Max.Mixing.Weight
The upper bound of the mixing ratio in Simple mixing and Kerker mixing. If convergence oscillates, setting it to a smaller value may stabilize the calculation.

scf.Mixing.History
The number of history steps used in Pulay-type mixing. A larger value provides more information, but larger is not always better. Even so, for many difficult cases, around 30 is a good starting point.

scf.Mixing.StartPulay
Specifies the SCF step at which Pulay-type mixing starts. Since switching to Pulay-type mixing from the very beginning can be unstable, a somewhat larger value, about 10 to 30, is effective for difficult cases.

scf.Mixing.EveryPulay
Specifies how often Pulay-type mixing is applied in RMM-DIISK, RMM-DIISV, and RMM-DIISH. For example, if 5 is specified, Pulay-type mixing is applied only once every five steps, and the basic mixing is used in the other steps. A value of 1 corresponds to the usual setting in which Pulay-type mixing is applied at every step.

scf.Kerker.factor
A parameter that determines the degree of suppression of the long-wavelength components in the Kerker method, RMM-DIISK, and RMM-DIISV. If it is not specified, it is automatically estimated. First use the automatic estimate, and specify it manually only when necessary.

scf.criterion
The convergence criterion for SCF calculations (in Hartree units). The SCF iteration stops when the absolute value of the band-energy difference between the current iteration and the previous iteration becomes smaller than this value. The default value is 1.0e-6.

How to read the output file

In the System.Name.out file, the following information is output for each SCF iteration, for example.

SCF= 1 NormRD= 1.000000000000 Uele= -3.523169099731
SCF= 2 NormRD= 0.181517404404 Uele= -3.686855123738
...

Here, NormRD is the residual norm, and Uele is the band energy. If the calculation is heading toward convergence, NormRD decreases overall and the change in Uele also becomes smaller. However, in Pulay-type mixing, NormRD may temporarily increase during the process. Do not immediately judge it as a failure just because it is not monotonically decreasing; instead, look at the trend over several steps.

Common pitfalls

  • •

    Setting the mixing ratio too large from the beginning. If the mixing ratio is made large in the hope of speeding up convergence, the calculation may instead diverge.

  • •

    Fixing scf.Kerker.factor from the beginning. First use the automatic estimation, and adjust it only when necessary.

  • •

    Starting Pulay-type mixing too early. In the initial stage of convergence, it is more stable first to reduce the residual to some extent by Simple or Kerker-type mixing and then switch to Pulay-type mixing.

  • •

    Setting the electronic temperature too low in metallic systems. This causes numerical instability in metallic systems.

  • •

    Relying only on electron-density mixing in the plus U method or in constraint methods. Even when the electron density is almost converged, the Hamiltonian containing the U term or constraint terms may continue to fluctuate. In that case, try RMM-DIISH or RMM-DIIS.

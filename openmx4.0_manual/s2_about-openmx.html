2 About OpenMX

OpenMX (Open source package for Material eXplorer) is an open-source software package for first-principles electronic structure calculations of materials based on density functional theory (DFT)[1], norm-conserving pseudopotentials[35, 36, 37, 38, 39], and optimized pseudo-atomic basis functions[44]. It can treat, within a unified framework, a wide range of systems including clusters, molecules, solids, surfaces, interfaces, low-dimensional materials, and liquids, together with their electronic structures, magnetic structures, geometrical structures, and various response functions. In addition to ground-state calculations, OpenMX implements functionalities frequently required in materials research, such as structural optimization, molecular dynamics, calculations under electric fields, charge doping, electrical transport calculations, core-level excitations, Wannier functions, and various charge and bonding analyses. These functionalities are closely connected to the understanding of magnetic properties, dielectric and optical responses, transport properties, chemical states at surfaces and interfaces, and reaction pathways. For example, calculations of exchange coupling parameters and the noncollinear DFT method are useful for analyzing magnetic interactions and magnetic states in layered magnetic materials and molecular magnets[17, 18, 19, 109], while calculations of optical conductivity and dielectric functions are used to evaluate optical responses based on electronic structures[108]. The nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) method is useful for analyzing the electrical transport properties of nanoscale junctions, and the interface to BoltzTraP/BoltzTraP2 is valuable for evaluating thermoelectric transport coefficients[81, 83, 112, 111]. Furthermore, the NEB method can be used to investigate reaction pathways and energy barriers for diffusion and proton transfer[79, 80], and calculations of core-electron binding energies are useful for interpreting XPS spectra[97, 103, 98].

The main features and functionalities of OpenMX are summarized below.

  • •

    Calculations of total energies and forces by the cluster approach, band-structure approach, O(N) method, and numerically exact low-order scaling methods

  • •

    Local density approximation (LDA, LSDA)[2, 3, 4] and generalized gradient approximation (GGA)[5] for exchange-correlation energies, DFT+U[21], norm-conserving pseudopotentials[2, 36, 37, 39], and optimized pseudo-atomic basis functions[44]

  • •

    Fully relativistic and scalar-relativistic treatments[12, 35, 16], noncollinear DFT[8, 9, 10, 11], and control of the directions of spin and orbital magnetic moments[13]

  • •

    Highly parallel eigenvalue calculations using ELPA[42] and ScaLAPACK, parallel computations with MPI and OpenMP, various interfaces for developers, and memory-leak analysis tools for developers

  • •

    Fully and constrained structural optimizations, variable-cell structural optimization including RFC5, NVE/NVT/NPT ensemble molecular dynamics[31, 32, 33, 34], and the NEB method[79]

  • •

    Analyses of wave functions, charge densities, spin densities, and surface electronic states by the regional chemical potential method[77]

  • •

    Flexible input-file formats and support for generating input files from CIF files by cif2omx

  • •

    Visualization support in conjunction with OpenMX Viewer [165, 164] and VESTA [115]

  • •

    Electrical transport calculations by the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) method[81] and an interface to BoltzTraP/BoltzTraP2[112, 111]

  • •

    Calculations of macroscopic polarization by the Berry-phase method[15], exchange coupling parameters[17, 18], and optical conductivity and dielectric functions[108]

  • •

    Generation of closest Wannier functions (CWFs) and maximally localized Wannier functions[133, 158], and an interface to Wannier90[158]

  • •

    COHP/COOP analyses[71, 72], density of states (DOS) and projected DOS, charge analyses by Mulliken, Voronoi, and ESP schemes[74], and natural orbital analysis[7]

  • •

    Effective screening medium (ESM) method[138, 141], introduction of a uniform electric field by a sawtooth potential, charge doping, and calculations of absolute core-electron binding energies[97]

In Ver. 4.0, computational methods that deepen the understanding of material structures and electronic states have been added, including closest Wannier functions (CWFs)[133], NPT molecular dynamics based on the Parrinello-Rahman and Wentzcovitch methods[33, 34], COHP/COOP analyses[71, 72], as well as analyses of material surfaces based on the regional chemical potential[77]. In addition, both new functionalities and extensions of existing ones have been advanced, such as a support tool for generating input files from CIF files, k-point sampling including the Γ point and time-reversal invariant momentum (TRIM) points, constrained variable-cell structural optimization by RFC5, the combined use of absolute core-electron binding-energy calculations with the ESM method, an interface to BoltzTraP/BoltzTraP2, interfacing external programs via the OpenMX API, development-support tools for post-processing codes using ELPA, and memory-leak analysis tools for developers.

The computational methods and program implementation of OpenMX have been designed with large-scale calculations on parallel computers in mind. Since databases of pseudopotentials and basis functions are available for many elements, users can quickly start simulations while concentrating on the validation of their own calculation conditions. In addition, OpenMX outputs can be visualized in conjunction with OpenMX Viewer [165, 164] and VESTA [115], allowing users to proceed with input preparation, validation of calculated results, and analyses while examining structures, molecular-dynamics trajectories, charge densities, wave functions, and band dispersions.

The development of OpenMX was initiated in 2000 by the Ozaki group, and it has subsequently been advanced by the many developers listed at the beginning of this manual. The program package and source code are distributed under the terms of the GNU General Public License version 3 (GPLv3)[114], and can be obtained from the OpenMX website. The development of OpenMX is still ongoing, and participation is welcome from those who are interested in improving and extending this open-source code. Examples of previous application studies and related information are available on the OpenMX website (http://www.openmx-square.org/), and readers are encouraged to consult them.

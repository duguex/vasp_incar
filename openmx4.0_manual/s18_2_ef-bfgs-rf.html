18.2 EF, BFGS, RF, and DIIS Optimization

Opt is a robust method, but in general its convergence is slow. As faster methods, geometry optimization schemes based on quasi-Newton methods are available. Eigenvector following (EF) method [66], Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) method [68], rational function (RF) method [67], and direct inversion in the iterative subspace (DIIS) method [65], which are carried out in Cartesian coordinates, are supported. In the EF and RF methods, the approximate Hessian is updated by the BFGS method. Please choose one of Opt, EF, BFGS, RF, and DIIS by the keyword MD.Type. The related keywords are as follows.

   MD.Type                     EF         # Opt|DIIS|BFGS|RF|EF
   MD.Opt.DIIS.History          3         # default=3
   MD.Opt.StartDIIS             5         # default=5
   MD.Opt.EveryDIIS            200        # default=200
   MD.maxIter                  100        # default=1
   MD.Opt.criterion          1.0e-4       # default=0.0003 (Hartree/Bohr)

In particular, these geometry optimization methods based on quasi-Newton schemes are controlled by the following two keywords.

   MD.Opt.DIIS.History      3       # default=3
   MD.Opt.StartDIIS         5       # default=5

The keyword MD.Opt.DIIS.History specifies the number of previous history steps used to update the approximate Hessian. The default value is 3. In addition, the geometry optimization step at which EF, BFGS, RF, and DIIS are started is specified by the keyword MD.Opt.StartDIIS. The geometry optimization steps before these methods are started are performed by the steepest descent method. The default value is 5. The DIIS method corresponds to an RMM-DIIS-type update in which the Hessian matrix is approximated by the identity matrix, whereas the BFGS method is the most basic quasi-Newton method that successively updates the approximate inverse Hessian. On the other hand, the RF method stabilizes the optimization step by using an augmented Hessian, and the EF method is designed to suppress instabilities caused by an inappropriate Hessian by monitoring and correcting the eigenvalues of the approximate Hessian.

The initial optimization step is automatically adjusted by referring to the maximum value (absolute value) of the force in the initial structure. Figure 11 shows the number of geometry optimization steps required for molecules and bulk systems until the maximum value (absolute value) of the force becomes 0.0003 Hartree/Bohr or less. As shown here, although the EF and BFGS methods exhibit similar performance, the RF method is the most robust and efficient one in most cases. The input and output files used in these calculations are located in the work/geoopt_example/ directory.

It should also be noted that these quasi-Newton methods may cause the structure to converge to a saddle point rather than to a local minimum. This may happen when the structure at which the quasi-Newton method is started has not yet passed an inflection point. In such a case, the structure should be sufficiently optimized by the steepest descent method before switching to the quasi-Newton method. The remedy is simply to use a larger value for MD.Opt.StartDIIS. For systems that require many iteration steps to achieve sufficient convergence, it may also be necessary to restart the calculation using the file System.Name.dat#, where System.Name is the System.Name specified in the input file. For stable optimization, it is also important not to update the approximate Hessian when the inner product ⟨Δ⁢x|Δ⁢g⟩ becomes negative, and always to monitor the maximum step length so as to avoid excessively large atomic displacements. The RF and EF methods are designed with this point in mind.

In general, by using a large value for scf.energycutoff, the energy curve becomes smoother, which can in turn accelerate convergence. This is particularly evident for weakly interacting systems such as molecular solids. For such weakly interacting systems, the use of a large scf.energycutoff of about 300–400 Ry is recommended.
